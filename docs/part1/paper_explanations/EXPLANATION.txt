PART 1 - THE PROBLEM
================================================================================

1.1  THE GAME

  Generals.io is a real-time strategy game on a grid, normally 23x23.

    - Each player starts with one "general" tile.
    - Every tile you own holds some number of army units.
    - Each turn ("tick") you pick ONE tile you own and ONE direction, and
      send either all-but-one unit or exactly half of them to the
      neighbouring tile.
    - If the destination is empty you take it. If it is the enemy's, the
      two stacks cancel one for one and the bigger one survives and owns
      the tile.
    - You win by moving an army onto the enemy general and winning that fight.

  Production, which matters more than it looks:

    - Your general and any "castle" you own produce +1 unit every 2 ticks.
    - EVERY tile you own produces +1 unit every 50 ticks.
    - Neutral castles sit on the map holding 40-50 units. Beat that garrison
      and the castle is yours, and it produces for you forever after.

  A tick is 0.5 seconds. Both players move at the same instant.


1.2  WHAT WE CHANGED, AND WHY IT IS A BIG DEAL

  In the real game there is fog of war: you only see tiles you own plus the
  ring around them. We removed it. Both players see the whole board.

  This is not a cosmetic change. It changes which algorithms are legal to use.

    WITH FOG                            WITHOUT FOG (our version)
    ------------------------------------------------------------------------
    You reason about a "belief state"   The state IS the board. What you see
    - a probability distribution over   is what is there. No inference layer.
    where the enemy might be.

    Minimax is unsound. You cannot      Minimax applies directly. You can
    evaluate a position you cannot      evaluate any position exactly.
    see.

    You can never PROVE anything        Forced wins become provable in
    about the opponent, only guess.     principle.

    Finding the enemy general is a      You know where it is from turn 0.
    whole research problem.

  There is a striking detail in the paper this project is based on. Their
  trained neural network's most interpretable component turned out to be a
  part of the network that maintains a guess about where the enemy general is.
  That is an expensive learned approximation of something WE CAN JUST READ OFF
  THE BOARD. Removing fog handed us for free the hardest thing their system
  had to learn.

  ONE THING IS STILL HIDDEN: simultaneity. You commit your move for tick t
  without seeing their move for tick t. That is the only imperfect information
  left, and it is what stops the game being a solved search problem.


1.3  THE CONSTRAINTS WE ARE UNDER

  (a) 0.5 seconds per move, forever.
      This rules out any algorithm that returns nothing until it finishes.
      The tick arrives whether you are ready or not.

  (b) Branching factor 40 to 400 per side.
      The raw action space is 23 x 23 x 9 = 4761 (nine actions per tile:
      pass, or two move types in four directions). Most are illegal at any
      moment, but 40-400 legal moves is still enormous for game-tree search.
      For comparison, chess averages about 35.

      Why this hurts: a game tree of depth d with branching b has roughly
      b^d nodes. Alpha-beta with perfect move ordering gets you down to
      b^(d/2). With b = 200 that is 200^1.5 = about 2,800 nodes for THREE
      ply. Sounds fine - until you remember each node needs a full game-state
      simulation and an evaluation. We measured ~0.6 ms to evaluate ~400
      moves in one batch, so three ply of naive search blows the budget.

  (c) Games last 300 to 1000 ticks, and the reward is only at the end.
      You win or you lose. There is no score along the way telling you
      whether move 137 was good.

  (d) Search and CSP only. AIMA chapters 3-6. No reinforcement learning,
      no neural networks, no machine learning of any kind.

  On (d): this is less limiting than it sounds. The strongest NON-LEARNING
  agent on the public Generals.io ladder is called Human.exe. It is built
  entirely out of classical optimisation - graph algorithms and dynamic
  programming - and it was the best bot in the world at this game until an
  RL agent trained on four H200 GPUs beat it. A search agent here is not a
  toy exercise.


1.4  WHERE WE ARE RIGHT NOW

  We have built:
    - A simulator of the modified game in JAX, fast enough to evaluate every
      candidate move in a single batched call (0.6 ms).
    - A pygame viewer to watch games and rewind them.
    - A first bot.

  THE FIRST BOT (strategies/greedy_eval.py) is one step of hill climbing,
  which is AIMA section 4.1.1:

      for every legal move:
          simulate the board after that move
          score it with an evaluation function
      play the move with the highest score

  Result: 20% win rate against a simple scripted opponent ("expander").

  We then instrumented 20 games to find out HOW it was losing:

      our general captured .......... 12 / 20     <-- the problem
      their general captured .........  3 / 20
      timeout, we were ahead .........  1 / 20
      timeout, they were ahead .......  4 / 20

  And we checked the land count: 27 to 27 at tick 300. DEAD EVEN.

  So it is not being out-expanded. It expands perfectly well and then gets
  killed. Why?

  Look at the simulate step. To simulate "the board after my move" you need
  BOTH players' moves, because they resolve simultaneously. The bot assumes
  the opponent passes. So every board it ever scores is a board in which the
  enemy did nothing.

      AN INCOMING ATTACK IS LITERALLY INVISIBLE TO IT.

  It cannot defend, because it has never once imagined being attacked.


1.5  THE FIX THAT DIDN'T WORK (read this, it shapes everything after)

  The obvious patch: add a "danger" term to the evaluation function. Find the
  enemy's biggest stack, measure how close it is to our general, and subtract
  points when it is close and large.

  We built it. Win rate went from 20% to 15%. IT MADE THE BOT WORSE.

  Here is why, and it is worth understanding properly because it constrains
  every later design decision.

  The bot chooses by argmax over its candidate moves. For a term to affect
  an argmax, it has to VARY between the candidates. The danger term depends
  almost entirely on where the ENEMY's stack is - and our own move does not
  move the enemy. So the term took nearly the same value for all 400
  candidates. It contributed no signal, only noise from the few cases where
  it did vary slightly.

      A TERM THAT DOES NOT VARY BETWEEN NEIGHBOURS CANNOT CHANGE AN ARGMAX.

  This is why we cannot fix the bot by adding more features to the evaluation
  function. The fix has to change WHAT WE SEARCH, not how we score.

  (Report this failure. It is more convincing than the successes, because it
  explains why the architecture is shaped the way it is.)

PAPER 1 - ARA*: ANYTIME A* WITH PROVABLE BOUNDS ON SUB-OPTIMALITY
Likhachev, Gordon & Thrun. NIPS 2003. 8 pages. AIMA chapter 3.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(1) THE IDEA AND THE MATHS

  Start from A*, which you know:

      f(n) = g(n) + h(n)

      g(n) = actual cost from the start to n
      h(n) = estimated cost from n to the goal

  A* always expands the node with the smallest f. If h never OVERESTIMATES
  the true remaining cost (this is called being "admissible"), A* is
  guaranteed to find the optimal path.

  The problem for us: A* gives you nothing until it finishes. If your
  deadline hits first, you have no answer at all.

  WEIGHTED A* is the classic fix. Multiply h by a number eps > 1:

      f(n) = g(n) + eps * h(n)

  Inflating h makes the search greedier - it rushes toward the goal instead
  of carefully exploring alternatives. It finds a path much faster. And here
  is the important part, which is a theorem and not a hope:

      the path it finds costs at most  eps x (the optimal cost)

  So eps = 2 means "at most twice optimal, but found much faster". You have
  a dial between speed and quality, with a guarantee at every setting.

  ARA*'s contribution: don't pick one eps. Run weighted A* repeatedly with
  eps decreasing - say 3.0, then 2.0, then 1.5, then 1.2, then 1.0 - and
  make each run REUSE the previous run's work instead of starting over.

  You get a rough answer almost immediately, it gets better the longer you
  let it run, and at every moment you know exactly how far from optimal you
  are. Stop whenever you like.

  THE MECHANISM THAT MAKES REUSE POSSIBLE

  This is the clever part. The paper introduces "local inconsistency".

  A state s is LOCALLY INCONSISTENT when its g-value has just been lowered
  and it has not been expanded since.

  Think about what that means. If g(s) drops, then s's successors might now
  be reachable more cheaply through s, but they don't know that yet. There
  is a mismatch - an inconsistency - between s and its children. Expanding s
  fixes it, by pushing the improvement down to the children (which then
  become inconsistent themselves).

  The paper's observation:

      OPEN IS EXACTLY THE SET OF LOCALLY INCONSISTENT STATES.

  Every time a g-value is lowered the state goes into OPEN; every time a
  state is expanded it comes out. So a search is nothing more than
  "propagate inconsistency until there is none left".

  Once you see it that way, resuming is obvious: to search again with a new
  eps, you do not restart, you just carry the still-inconsistent states
  forward.

  THE COMPLICATION, AND WHY THERE IS A THIRD LIST

  With eps > 1 the heuristic can be inconsistent, which means A* might want
  to expand the same state many times. That is expensive. ARA* forbids
  re-expanding a state within one pass - this is what keeps each pass cheap
  and keeps the eps guarantee.

  But a state whose g improves AFTER it was already expanded is genuinely
  inconsistent, and forgetting it would be wrong. So it goes into a third
  list called INCONS. At the end of the pass, INCONS gets poured back into
  OPEN for the next round.

      OPEN    - inconsistent states we are allowed to expand this pass
      CLOSED  - already expanded this pass, do not touch again
      INCONS  - inconsistent, but already expanded; deal with them next pass

  THE PSEUDOCODE

      fvalue(s) = g(s) + eps * h(s)

      ImprovePath():
        while fvalue(goal) > min fvalue(s) over s in OPEN:
            s = pop the smallest fvalue from OPEN
            CLOSED = CLOSED + {s}
            for each successor s' of s:
                if g(s') > g(s) + cost(s, s'):
                    g(s') = g(s) + cost(s, s')
                    if s' not in CLOSED:  put s' in OPEN
                    else:                 put s' in INCONS      <-- the trick

      Main():
        g(start) = 0;  everything else infinity
        put start in OPEN
        ImprovePath()
        publish the solution
        while the bound is still worse than 1:
            decrease eps
            move everything from INCONS into OPEN               <-- the reuse
            re-sort OPEN with the new eps
            CLOSED = empty
            ImprovePath()
            publish the improved solution

  THE BOUND YOU ACTUALLY GET

  Notice the published bound is not eps. It is:

                                  g(goal)
      eps' = min( eps ,  ------------------------------ )
                          min over s in OPEN + INCONS
                             of  ( g(s) + h(s) )

  The denominator is a lower bound on the true optimal cost - it is the
  cheapest anything still under consideration could possibly be. So this
  ratio is often much tighter than eps itself. In practice you frequently
  have a near-optimal answer long before eps has come down to 1.


(2) WHY IT IS USEFUL HERE

  Because "0.5 seconds, then answer, no excuses" is precisely the situation
  ARA* was designed for. Any algorithm we use has to be interruptible. ARA*
  is interruptible AND tells you how good the answer you are holding is.

  Second use, quite separate: THE OPENING. For roughly the first 34 ticks
  the two players cannot interact at all - generals start at least 17 steps
  apart and a stack needs time to grow before it can travel. So "how much
  land can I own by tick 50" is a single-player, deterministic,
  no-opponent shortest-path problem. That is a pure chapter-3 exercise, and
  it is worth real strength: openings decide a lot of Generals.io games.

  We can even bound it. A lone general holds 1 + floor(T/2) army at tick T
  (it makes one unit every two ticks). Every tile you capture parks exactly
  one unit on it permanently, and each capture costs one tick. So:

      land at tick 50  <=  1 + floor(50/2)  =  26

  Good human openings reach 23-25. So the bound is tight, which is exactly
  what makes branch-and-bound search efficient here.


(3) WHAT YOU TRANSLATE

  The anytime loop, directly:

      deadline = now + 400ms
      best = the greedy move            # so we ALWAYS have an answer

      for eps in [3.0, 2.0, 1.5, 1.2, 1.0]:
          if now > deadline: break
          best = search(eps)            # reusing the previous iteration
      return best

  And the reporting habit. Most projects can only claim "it plays well".
  ARA* lets you write "within 12% of optimal, in 400 ms". That is a number.


(4) CONSIDERATIONS

  * ARA* assumes the graph does not change and the goal is fixed. Our board
    changes every single tick. So the reuse is only valid WITHIN one tick's
    thinking. You cannot carry an ARA* search across ticks. For that you
    need paper 2.

  * The eps schedule matters and is worth tuning. Too coarse and you waste
    a whole pass; too fine and you never finish a pass.

  * "Anytime" only helps if the first answer is decent. Seed it with the
    greedy move so the worst case is "we did no worse than the current bot".

PAPER 2 - REAL-TIME ADAPTIVE A* (RTAA*)
Koenig & Likhachev. AAMAS 2006. 8 pages. AIMA section 4.5.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(1) THE IDEA AND THE MATHS

  The setting: an agent that must ACT NOW, cannot search all the way to the
  goal, and will be back in a similar situation next step. Your book covers
  this in section 4.5 under "online search agents", and the algorithm it
  teaches is LRTA* - Learning Real-Time A*.

  LRTA* works like this. You keep a table h(s) of estimates. Each step:

      1. look one step ahead (or a few)
      2. move toward the most promising neighbour
      3. UPDATE the estimate for the state you were in:

             h(s) = max( h(s),  min over actions a of [ cost(s,a,s') + h(s') ] )

  That update is the whole idea. If all your neighbours turn out to be
  further from the goal than you thought, then you were too optimistic about
  where you are standing, so raise h(s). Over repeated visits the table
  becomes more and more accurate, and the agent stops making the same
  mistake twice. Note this is NOT machine learning - it is an inequality
  being enforced, with no model being fitted.

  The weakness: LRTA* updates essentially one state per move. On a big map
  that is slow going - the agent has to physically walk around correcting
  one number at a time.

  RTAA*'S CONTRIBUTION IS ONE LINE.

  Instead of updating one state, run a bounded A* search, then update EVERY
  STATE THAT SEARCH EXPANDED, all at once, with one subtraction each.

      while not at goal:
          lookahead = any integer > 0        # how much to think
          run A*, expanding at most `lookahead` states
          s_bar = the state left in OPEN with the smallest f
                  (i.e. the frontier state A* was about to expand next)

          for every state s that was expanded (i.e. in CLOSED):
              h(s) = g(s_bar) + h(s_bar) - g(s)          <-- THE WHOLE PAPER

          movements = any integer > 0        # how much to commit to
          take that many steps along the path toward s_bar

  WHY THAT UPDATE IS LEGAL

  This is worth being able to explain, because it is the bit that makes the
  algorithm sound.

  A* expands states in order of increasing f. So when it stops, the smallest
  f left in OPEN - call that state s_bar - gives you f(s_bar) = g(s_bar) +
  h(s_bar), and this is a LOWER BOUND on the true distance from where you
  are standing to the goal. (Nothing cheaper than that is still available.)

  Now take any state s that the search expanded. To get from s to the goal
  you must leave the searched region, and to leave it you must pass through
  the frontier. So:

      true distance from s to goal   >=   f(s_bar) - g(s)

  ...and the right-hand side is exactly what we assign to h(s). Since it is
  a lower bound, the new h is still admissible - it never overestimates -
  so everything A* guarantees still holds.

  The paper also proves the update is MONOTONICALLY NON-DECREASING: h only
  ever goes up. Which means the table only ever gets more informed. It
  cannot get worse.

  RTAA* VERSUS LRTA*

      LRTA*:  more expensive update, produces a better-informed h
      RTAA*:  one subtraction per state, produces a slightly worse h

  The paper is honest that LRTA*'s heuristic ends up MORE informed for the
  same lookahead. But RTAA* is so much cheaper per move that it reaches the
  goal sooner in wall-clock terms.

  That trade-off is exactly ours. We are limited by TIME, not by number of
  moves. So we want the cheap update.


(2) WHY IT IS USEFUL HERE

  This is the shape of our entire tick loop. "Think as much as 500 ms
  allows, commit to something, keep what you learned, repeat forever."
  That is RTAA* written out in English.

  And unlike ARA*, RTAA* is DESIGNED for an environment that changes
  underneath you. The h table is meant to survive between decisions. That is
  the thing we actually need and cannot get from paper 1.


(3) WHAT YOU TRANSLATE

  Define the table:

      h(tile) = estimated number of ticks until I own this tile

  Initialise it as BFS distance from our current territory - a tile two
  steps from our border starts at h = 2.

  Then every tick, RTAA* raises the values of tiles that turned out to be
  harder than they looked. A tile guarded by a big enemy stack gets its h
  pushed up, and STOPS ATTRACTING OUR ARMY. Over a few hundred ticks the
  table becomes a map of "where it is actually worth going on THIS board",
  which is different for every game.

  Implementation is small: one A* with a cap on expansions, one loop over
  CLOSED. Maybe 60 lines on top of a pathfinder we need anyway.


(4) CONSIDERATIONS

  * The two lines that say "any integer > 0" are LEFT UNDEFINED BY THE
    PAPER. It genuinely does not tell you how much to think or how far to
    commit. Choosing those two numbers well is the subject of paper 5. Do
    not skip past this - it is where our original contribution lives.

  * h must start admissible or the guarantees evaporate. BFS distance
    ignoring enemies is safe, because the true time to own a tile is always
    at least the distance to walk there.

  * Careful about what "goal" means. In pathfinding there is one goal tile.
    In our game the goal is a whole situation ("own more land", "kill the
    general"). Simplest workable version: run the h table for a concrete
    sub-goal like "reach the enemy general", and keep a separate one for
    "reach the nearest unowned tile".

  * When territory flips, some h values become stale. Because updates only
    ever raise h, a tile that becomes EASIER will keep an over-large h until
    something resets it. Consider resetting h for tiles whose ownership
    changed.

PAPER 3 - DEPRESSION AVOIDANCE IN REAL-TIME HEURISTIC SEARCH
Hernandez & Baier. AAAI 2012. 6 pages. AIMA section 4.5.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(1) THE IDEA AND THE MATHS

  This paper is about the specific way real-time search FAILS, and a cheap
  fix. There is barely any maths, which is a good sign - it is an
  observation, not a construction.

  A HEURISTIC DEPRESSION is a connected region of states where h badly
  underestimates the true cost, compared to the h values on the region's
  border.

  Picture the heuristic as a landscape where low means "close to the goal".
  A depression is a bowl. A greedy agent rolls in, because from outside
  everything in the bowl looks close to the goal. Then it is stuck: every
  move it makes raises one state's h a little, so it wanders around the bowl
  raising values one at a time until the whole bowl has been filled in and
  it can finally climb out.

  This wastes an enormous number of moves. The literature calls it
  "scrubbing" - the agent scrubs back and forth over the same region.

  THE FIX, IN ONE SENTENCE:

      Prefer to move to states that are not known to be inside a depression.

  DETECTION IS FREE

  You do not need a detector. Here is the observation that makes it cheap:

      If a state's h has ever been UPDATED, that state is inside a depression.

  Why: h starts out admissible (never overestimating), and updates only ever
  RAISE it (proved in the RTAA* paper). So if we ever had to raise h(s), the
  original estimate at s was too optimistic. That is the definition of a
  depression. One extra bit per state:

      h0(s) = h(s)                    # remember the original value, once
      ...
      marked(s) = ( h(s) > h0(s) )    # true means "we were wrong here before"

  TWO WAYS TO USE THE MARK

  MARK-AND-AVOID. When choosing which frontier state to head for, prefer
  unmarked ones. Only fall back to marked ones if everything is marked.
  Implementation: sort the frontier by (marked, f) instead of by f alone.

  MOVE-TO-BORDER. Softer. If you are already deep inside a depression and
  every neighbour is marked, head for the states that look closest to the
  EDGE of the depression - aim for the exit rather than refusing to be in
  the bowl at all.


(2) WHY IT IS USEFUL HERE

  Because we already hit this, and we have the numbers.

  Our greedy bot picks the move whose resulting board scores highest. It
  wins 20%. It loses by having its general captured 12 times in 20, while
  its land count stays even. It walks into positions that look good one move
  out and are fatal two moves out. THAT IS A HEURISTIC DEPRESSION. This
  paper is the named, published treatment of the failure we measured.

  What a depression looks like on OUR board, concretely:

    - A corridor of neutral tiles that dead-ends against mountains. Every
      single step in looks like progress (land goes up by one). The whole
      excursion was wasted, and you find out only at the end.

    - Tiles adjacent to a large enemy stack. They score well, we take them,
      the enemy takes them straight back, we take them again. We re-lose the
      same tiles repeatedly. This is textbook scrubbing.


(3) WHAT YOU TRANSLATE

  Once the RTAA* h table exists, this is roughly ten lines:

      when you raise h(tile):     marked[tile] = true
      when choosing where to go:  prefer tiles with marked == false

  And it is a clean experiment: run 200 games with marking on and off,
  report the difference. That is a real result with a real number.


(4) CONSIDERATIONS

  * This ONLY works if you have already built the h table (paper 2). It is
    not a standalone technique - it piggybacks on the update mechanism.

  * Marks should probably decay or reset. Our board changes; a tile that was
    a trap 200 ticks ago may be free now because the enemy stack moved away.
    A permanent mark would slowly make the whole board look like a trap.
    Simple fix: clear the mark when the tile's neighbourhood ownership
    changes.

  * IMPORTANT CONTRAST WITH OUR FAILED DANGER TERM. Both are "avoid bad
    places". Why should this one work when that one made things worse?

    Because the danger term was nearly the same for every candidate move, so
    it could not discriminate. A depression mark is attached to THE STATES
    WE ARE CHOOSING BETWEEN - move A leads to a marked tile, move B does
    not - so it varies exactly where it needs to. That is the difference,
    and it is worth saying out loud in the report.

PAPER 4 - PROBCUT: FORWARD PRUNING IN ALPHA-BETA
Buro. AIMA sections 5.3-5.4.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(1) THE IDEA AND THE MATHS

  Background you need first. In a game tree, alpha-beta pruning maintains a
  window (alpha, beta):

      alpha = the best score I am already guaranteed
      beta  = the best score the opponent is already guaranteed

  If while searching a subtree you discover its value must fall outside that
  window, you can stop searching it - it can never affect the final choice.
  This is SOUND pruning: alpha-beta returns exactly what full minimax would.

  Soundness is expensive. It only prunes when it can PROVE irrelevance, and
  proof requires actually searching. The result is that depth stays low, and
  depth is what actually makes a game program strong.

  PROBCUT'S IDEA: prune when a subtree is PROBABLY irrelevant.

  Before searching a subtree to full depth d, do a cheap SHALLOW search to
  depth d' (much smaller). If the shallow result comes back far outside the
  window, conclude that the deep search would almost certainly also land
  outside, and skip it entirely.

  THE STATISTICAL MODEL

  Let v be the value a depth-d search would return, and v' the value a
  depth-d' search returns. These are strongly correlated - a position that
  looks winning after 2 ply usually looks winning after 6. Model it as a
  straight line with noise:

      v  =  a * v'  +  b  +  e,        where e is Gaussian with std dev sigma

  You get a, b and sigma by ORDINARY LINEAR REGRESSION over thousands of
  positions, done once, offline. This is least squares, not machine learning
  in the sense your course excludes - it is fitting a line to a scatter plot.

  THE PRUNING CONDITION, DERIVED

  We want to prune when the probability that v >= beta is high. Substitute
  the model:

      v >= beta
      a*v' + b + e >= beta
      v' >= (beta - b - e) / a

  We do not know e, but we know its standard deviation. Require the shallow
  value to clear the bar by T standard deviations, where T is a threshold we
  choose:

      PRUNE (fail high) when   v'  >=  ( T*sigma + beta - b ) / a

  And symmetrically at the bottom of the window:

      PRUNE (fail low)  when   v'  <=  ( -T*sigma + alpha - b ) / a

  Straight from the paper's own code:

      bound = round(( T*sigma + beta - b) / a);
      if (shallow_search(S, bound-1, bound) >= bound)  return beta;

      bound = round((-T*sigma + alpha - b) / a);
      if (shallow_search(S, bound, bound+1) <= bound)  return alpha;

  Note the shallow searches use a NULL WINDOW (bound-1, bound). They only
  need to answer "above or below?", never compute an exact value, which
  makes them very cheap.

  T IS THE RISK DIAL

      large T  ->  prune only when very confident  ->  safe, shallow, slow
      small T  ->  prune aggressively              ->  much deeper, sometimes
                                                       throws away the best move

  Typical values are around 1 to 1.5 standard deviations.


(2) WHY IT IS USEFUL HERE

  Our branching factor makes sound pruning insufficient. Perfect alpha-beta
  ordering gives about b^(d/2); with b around 200, three ply is already at
  the edge of the budget. Forward pruning is not a refinement for us - it is
  the only realistic route to useful depth.

  And here is a piece of luck: OUR SHALLOW SEARCH IS ALREADY BUILT AND
  NEARLY FREE. The JAX simulator evaluates every candidate move in ONE
  batched call, measured at 0.6 ms. That is exactly the cheap estimator
  ProbCut needs, and we get it from work already done for the greedy bot.

  Fitting a, b and sigma is a nice self-contained experiment: generate a few
  thousand positions from self-play, run a 1-ply and a 3-ply search on each,
  plot one against the other, fit a line. That produces a REAL FIGURE for
  the report - a scatter plot with a fitted line and a sigma band.


(3) WHAT YOU TRANSLATE

  Two things:

  (a) The pruning condition itself, inside whatever alpha-beta you write.

  (b) More generally, the PRINCIPLE: a cheap estimate can decide whether an
      expensive computation is worth doing. Hold onto that sentence - it is
      the same idea as paper 5, applied at a different level.


(4) CONSIDERATIONS

  * PROBCUT IS UNSOUND. It can and will prune the winning move sometimes.
    You must say this in the report; do not pretend otherwise. The standard
    mitigation: never forward-prune within k ply of a terminal position (a
    general capture), because that is exactly where a mistake cannot be
    recovered from.

  * The regression is only valid for the (d', d) pair you fitted it on. If
    you fit 1-ply against 3-ply, you cannot use those numbers for 2-ply
    against 6-ply. Fit each pair you intend to use.

  * Our evaluation function is currently weak. ProbCut's model assumes the
    shallow and deep searches are correlated - if your evaluation is noise,
    they will not be, sigma will be huge, and no pruning will ever fire.
    So this paper depends on having a decent evaluation first. Do not
    attempt it in week 1.

PAPER 5 - MIN-CONFLICTS: HEURISTIC REPAIR FOR CSPs
Minton, Johnston, Philips & Laird. AAAI 1990. 8 pages. AIMA section 6.4.
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

(1) THE IDEA AND THE MATHS

  A constraint satisfaction problem is: variables, each with a domain of
  possible values, and constraints saying which combinations are allowed.

  The standard approach in your book (section 6.3) is CONSTRUCTIVE: start
  with nothing assigned, assign one variable at a time, and backtrack when
  you paint yourself into a corner.

  Min-conflicts is REPAIR-BASED, which is the opposite:

      Start with a COMPLETE assignment that is probably broken.
      Repeatedly pick a variable that is currently violating a constraint,
      and change it to the value that violates the FEWEST constraints.
      Break ties randomly.

  The heuristic, in the paper's own words:

      "Given a variable in conflict, choose the value that minimises the
       number of other variables that will need to be repaired."

  Pseudocode:

      min_conflicts(problem, max_steps):
          assignment = some complete assignment
          repeat max_steps times:
              if assignment satisfies everything: return it
              var = a variable currently in conflict (pick randomly)
              val = the value for var minimising the number of conflicts
              assignment[var] = val        # ties broken randomly
          return failure

  WHY IT WORKS SO WELL

  The intuition the paper gives: a broken complete assignment is usually
  ALMOST RIGHT. It contains a huge amount of information - it is a solution
  to a slightly different problem. Constructive search throws all of that
  away by starting from an empty assignment. Repair keeps it.

  The results are famous. It solves the million-queens problem in seconds.
  It was the scheduler used for HUBBLE SPACE TELESCOPE observations, where a
  week of telescope time had to be planned under heavy constraints.

  It is NOT complete - it can cycle forever and it cannot prove that a
  problem has no solution. The random tie-breaking is what keeps it out of
  loops in practice.

  A SECOND USE THAT IS EASY TO MISS

  The paper also presents min-conflicts as a VALUE-ORDERING HEURISTIC inside
  ordinary backtracking search ("informed backtracking"): use the current
  broken assignment as a hint about which value to try first. So it is not
  only a local search method - it is a way of injecting a starting guess
  into a systematic search.


(2) WHY IT IS USEFUL HERE

  Because our real per-tick decision is not "what is the single best move".
  It is "WHICH OF MY STACKS DOES WHAT". That is an assignment problem:

      Variables    one per army stack we control
      Domains      { expand toward region R, gather to the general,
                     defend tile T, take castle C, intercept enemy stack S }
      Constraints  - two stacks should not be sent to the same target
                   - if the general is threatened, SOME stack must be able
                     to reach it in time
                   - a stack's army must exceed what its task requires

  And crucially: THIS PROBLEM BARELY CHANGES BETWEEN TICKS. One stack has
  arrived somewhere, one new threat has appeared, everything else is
  identical. Solving it from scratch twice a second is absurd. Repairing
  last tick's answer is exactly right, and that is what min-conflicts does.

  THE PART I THINK IS GENUINELY NOVEL: unsatisfiability is a SIGNAL, not
  just a failure. If constraint propagation wipes out a variable's domain -
  no stack can possibly reach that threatened tile in time - then we are
  PROVABLY losing that tile. That is a much stronger statement than an
  evaluation function's opinion, and it should feed straight into the
  evaluation as a term. I have not seen constraint propagation used as an
  evaluation feature in the RTS search literature.


(3) WHAT YOU TRANSLATE

  The repair loop, seeded from last tick's assignment. And the constraint
  "the general's threat must be covered", which addresses our measured
  failure DECLARATIVELY - by stating a requirement - rather than by adding
  another weighted term to an evaluation function, which we tried and which
  made things worse.


(4) CONSIDERATIONS

  * Do not let the CSP layer and the search layer fight each other. Keep the
    division clean: THE CSP PROPOSES a small set of coherent candidate
    plans; THE SEARCH CHOOSES between them. If both try to pick the final
    move you will get incoherent behaviour.

  * Min-conflicts is incomplete. It can fail to find an assignment that
    exists. With a 0.5 s budget, cap max_steps and accept the best
    assignment found. Do not treat failure as proof of impossibility unless
    you ran full constraint propagation (AC-3) first.

  * Defining the domains well is most of the work. "Expand toward region R"
    needs regions, which needs the board segmented into blobs. That is a
    piece of engineering, not a paper idea, and it will take longer than you
    expect.

PAPER 6 - METAREASONING: DECIDING HOW MUCH TO THINK
O'Ceallaigh & Ruml, SoCS 2015 (9pp)
Cserna, Ruml & Frank, ICAPS 2017 (5pp)
Russell & Wefald, UAI 1990 (7pp) - the same Russell as your textbook
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

  NOTE: this direction came from YOUR observation - that most ticks in a
  Generals.io game are obvious, so spending the full budget on them is
  waste. That intuition turned out to be an established subfield with
  thirty years of literature. Say that in the presentation; it is a good
  thing to have noticed independently.


(1) THE IDEA AND THE MATHS

  Ordinary search asks: "which ACTION should I take?"
  Metareasoning asks the level-up question: "which COMPUTATION should I
  perform - and is any computation worth doing at all right now?"

  Russell & Wefald gave the framework in 1990. Treat each possible node
  expansion as an action in its own right, with:

      a COST    - the time it takes
      a BENEFIT - the probability that doing it CHANGES WHICH MOVE YOU
                  FINALLY PLAY, times how much better that move is

  Then expand the node with the highest "value of computation", and STOP
  SEARCHING when the expected benefit of another expansion falls below its
  cost.

  The key sentence, which is the whole idea:

      A COMPUTATION THAT CANNOT CHANGE YOUR ANSWER HAS ZERO VALUE,
      NO MATTER HOW DEEP IT IS.

  Their algorithm applying this to game trees is called DTA* - Decision
  Theoretic A*. The unit of computation is a single node expansion, which
  is why the paper is called "fine-grained".

  THE 2015 PAPER: TWO CONCRETE DECISIONS

  O'Ceallaigh & Ruml apply this to real-time search and identify two
  decision points. And here is the thing that makes this fit our project so
  neatly: THEY ARE EXACTLY THE TWO LINES RTAA* LEFT UNDEFINED.

  Remember from paper 2:

      lookahead  = any integer > 0        <-- decision 1: how much to think
      movements  = any integer > 0        <-- decision 2: how much to commit

  DECISION 1 - THE IDENTITY ACTION

  An "identity action" is an action that keeps you in the same state while
  you think longer. The question: should I act now, or stand still and
  deliberate?

  Standard real-time search never asks this. It assumes acting is always
  right. But if your current best move is only marginally better than the
  second best, another round of lookahead might reveal something much
  better, and standing still only costs time.

  They measure everything in GAT - Goal Achievement Time - which is planning
  time PLUS execution time. Under GAT, thinking is not free, but it is often
  cheaper than committing to a bad move and having to undo it.

  DECISION 2 - PREFIX COMMITMENT

  After lookahead you have a path to the best frontier state. How much of
  that path do you actually execute before re-planning?

  The existing algorithms are both extremes, and neither adapts:

      LRTA* (Korf 1990)      commits to exactly 1 action
      Later variants (2013)  commit to the ENTIRE path to the frontier

  One action means re-planning constantly, which is expensive. The whole
  path means acting on stale information, which is dangerous. The paper
  chooses dynamically: commit further when the plan looks robust, commit
  less when it looks fragile.


(2) WHY IT IS USEFUL HERE

  Three reasons, and the second one is unusual.

  FIRST: it directly answers your observation. Most ticks are obvious.
  During a long expansion phase, one move is clearly best and we currently
  burn 500 ms confirming it.

  SECOND, AND THIS IS THE LUCKY BIT: Generals.io has a REAL identity action.
  Action index 0 on every tile is "pass". You can literally stand still and
  think. Most domains have to simulate an identity action or argue by
  analogy - we have one in the rules.

  THIRD: the game has a MOVE QUEUE. You can queue up a path of moves in
  advance and the server executes them one per tick. That is prefix
  commitment implemented for us by the game itself.

  So both of the paper's decisions have a direct, literal counterpart in our
  domain. That is unusual and it is why I think this is where our
  contribution is.


(3) WHAT YOU TRANSLATE

  DECISION 1, and this is genuinely about ten lines:

      scores = evaluate_all_moves_batched()      # 0.6 ms, we already have it

      if best_score - second_best_score > margin:
          play the best move immediately          # not close - deep search is waste
      else:
          spend the remaining ~490 ms searching properly

  DECISION 2:

      if the planned path runs entirely through OUR OWN territory:
          queue the whole path, skip planning for that many ticks
      else if the path approaches contested tiles:
          commit one move, re-plan next tick

  A stack walking 12 tiles home to the general is a robust plan - nothing
  can go wrong, so commit all 12 and think about something else for 12
  ticks. A stack walking into contact is fragile - commit one step.


(4) CONSIDERATIONS

  * The margin threshold has to be tuned, and there is a real risk of
    getting it wrong in the dangerous direction: skipping deep search on a
    tick that LOOKED obvious but was actually critical. Mitigation: never
    skip the search when the enemy has a stack within k tiles of our
    general, regardless of the margin.

  * Measuring the benefit honestly is harder than it looks. The clean
    experiment is: FIX THE TOTAL COMPUTE BUDGET FOR A WHOLE GAME, and vary
    how it is spread across ticks. If metareasoned allocation beats uniform
    allocation at the SAME total compute, that is a clean result. If you
    just make the bot faster you have proved nothing interesting.

  * Do not over-claim novelty. Metareasoning is thirty years old. What is
    (as far as I can find) new is applying identity actions and prefix
    commitment in a SIMULTANEOUS-MOVE ADVERSARIAL game with a domain that
    supplies both mechanics natively. Claim that, not more.

PART 3 - THE INSIGHTS, PULLED OUT
================================================================================

Here is everything above compressed into numbered insights you can actually
use. Each one says where it came from.

  I1.  ANYTIME IS MANDATORY, NOT OPTIONAL.
       We must return a move in 500 ms whether or not we are finished. Every
       algorithm has to be interruptible with a usable answer at all times.
                                                              [ARA*]

  I2.  A LOOSE ANSWER NOW BEATS A PERFECT ANSWER LATE - AND YOU CAN KNOW HOW
       LOOSE. Inflating the heuristic by eps gives a path at most eps times
       optimal. That is a guarantee, so "good enough" becomes measurable
       rather than hand-wavy.
                                                              [ARA*]

  I3.  NEVER THROW AWAY COMPUTED WORK.
       ARA* carries INCONS between passes. RTAA* carries the h table between
       ticks. Min-conflicts repairs last tick's assignment. On a board that
       barely changes between decisions, this is THE organising principle of
       the whole agent.
                                          [ARA*, RTAA*, min-conflicts]

  I4.  A HEURISTIC CAN IMPROVE ITSELF WITHOUT ANY LEARNING.
       h(s) = g(s_bar) + h(s_bar) - g(s) is an admissible update derived
       from an inequality. The bot gets better at the specific map it is
       playing, with no model, no training, no data.
                                                              [RTAA*]

  I5.  IF YOU HAD TO RAISE h SOMEWHERE, THAT PLACE IS A TRAP.
       Free depression detection: one bit per tile. Avoid marked tiles.
                                                  [depression avoidance]

  I6.  A TERM THAT DOES NOT VARY BETWEEN CANDIDATES CANNOT CHANGE AN ARGMAX.
       This is why our danger term made the bot worse (20% -> 15%), and why
       depression marks should work where it failed. Anything you add to the
       evaluation must DISCRIMINATE between the moves you are choosing among.
                                       [our own failed experiment]

  I7.  A CHEAP ESTIMATE CAN DECIDE WHETHER AN EXPENSIVE COMPUTATION IS WORTH
       DOING. ProbCut uses a shallow search to skip a deep one. Metareasoning
       uses a 1-ply scan to skip the whole search. SAME IDEA, TWO LEVELS.
                                              [ProbCut, metareasoning]

  I8.  SOUND PRUNING IS NOT ENOUGH AT b = 400. We have to accept a small,
       quantified probability of error to get depth. T sigma is the dial.
                                                              [ProbCut]

  I9.  REPAIR BEATS REBUILD WHEN THE PROBLEM BARELY CHANGED.
       Last tick's stack assignment is nearly right. Fix the two things that
       broke instead of re-solving from an empty assignment.
                                                       [min-conflicts]

  I10. UNSATISFIABILITY IS INFORMATION, NOT JUST FAILURE.
       If no assignment can cover a threat, we are PROVABLY losing that tile.
       Feed that into the evaluation. This is stronger than any heuristic
       opinion, and I have not seen it done in this literature.
                                          [min-conflicts, our idea]

  I11. MOST TICKS ARE OBVIOUS. Spending equal time on every tick is the
       single biggest waste in the current design.
                                            [metareasoning, your idea]

  I12. THE GAME HANDS US BOTH METAREASONING MECHANICS FOR FREE.
       "pass" is a genuine identity action. The move queue is genuine prefix
       commitment. We do not have to simulate either.
                                            [metareasoning, our domain]

  I13. CHANGE WHAT YOU SEARCH, NOT HOW YOU SCORE.
       Our bot cannot defend because it never simulates the opponent moving.
       No evaluation term fixes that. Only searching the opponent's replies
       fixes it.
                                       [our own failed experiment]

  I14. REMOVING FOG IS WHAT MAKES ALL OF THIS LEGAL.
       Minimax, provable statements, exact evaluation - none of it is sound
       with fog. Our modification is not a simplification, it is the enabling
       assumption.
                                                        [our own design]

This is the part to understand. It is one agent, and each paper occupies a
specific slot in it.

4.1  THE ONE-LINE VERSION

  Every tick: cheaply decide whether this tick is worth thinking about; if it
  is, use a CSP to propose a few coherent plans, search over those plans with
  an anytime adversarial search, and commit as far ahead as the plan is
  trustworthy. Carry everything you learned into the next tick.

4.3  WHY IT IS ORDERED THIS WAY

  Notice the funnel. Each step is cheaper than the one after it, and each one
  can terminate the tick early:

      Step 0    0 ms      queued move        -> exits most ticks in a march
      Step 2    0.6 ms    1-ply scan         -> exits most "obvious" ticks
      Step 3    20 ms     CSP repair         -> narrows 400 moves to 4-8 plans
      Step 4    450 ms    the real search    -> only runs when it matters

  That ordering is itself the metareasoning principle applied to the
  architecture, not just to one decision: NEVER PAY FOR A COMPUTATION UNTIL
  A CHEAPER ONE HAS FAILED TO ANSWER THE QUESTION.


4.4  HOW THE PAPERS COMBINE - THE SHORT ANSWER

  You asked for something like "metareasoning using ARA*, RTAA* for the
  metareasoned decision, ProbCut, CSP somewhere". Close. Here is the exact
  relationship:

      METAREASONING is the GATE.
          It decides whether we search at all, and how far we commit.
          It sits OUTSIDE everything else.

      THE CSP is the PROPOSER.
          It converts 400 raw moves into a handful of coherent plans, and
          reports when a threat is uncoverable.
          It runs BEFORE the search and shrinks its input.

      ARA* is the SHAPE OF THE SEARCH.
          The anytime loop with decreasing eps. It makes the search
          interruptible and gives us a quality bound.

      PROBCUT lives INSIDE the search.
          At each node, it decides whether to go deep. Same idea as the
          metareasoning gate, one level down.

      RTAA* is the MEMORY.
          It sits underneath all of it, supplying the h values the
          evaluation uses, and improving them every tick.

      DEPRESSION AVOIDANCE is a CORRECTION on RTAA*.
          One bit per tile, consumed by the evaluation.

  In one sentence:

      METAREASONING decides IF we think,
      the CSP decides WHAT we think about,
      ARA* decides HOW LONG we think,
      PROBCUT decides HOW DEEP within that,
      and RTAA* is what we REMEMBER between thoughts.

PART 6 - GLOSSARY
================================================================================

  These are the terms you will need to say out loud. Short definitions.

  ADMISSIBLE HEURISTIC
      An h that never overestimates the true remaining cost. Required for A*
      to be optimal.

  ALPHA-BETA PRUNING
      Skipping subtrees of a game tree that provably cannot affect the final
      choice, tracked with a window (alpha, beta). Sound - returns exactly
      what full minimax would.

  ANYTIME ALGORITHM
      One that always has a valid answer available, which improves the longer
      it runs. You can stop it at any moment.

  BRANCHING FACTOR (b)
      The average number of legal moves at a position. Ours is 40-400;
      chess is about 35.

  FORWARD PRUNING
      Cutting a subtree that MIGHT have mattered, based on a cheap estimate.
      Unsound but much more aggressive than alpha-beta. ProbCut is this.

  GAT (GOAL ACHIEVEMENT TIME)
      Planning time plus execution time. The metric that makes "should I
      think longer?" a well-posed question.

  HEURISTIC DEPRESSION
      A region where h underestimates badly compared to its border. A bowl in
      the heuristic landscape that traps greedy agents.

  IDENTITY ACTION
      An action that leaves you in the same state, so you can think longer.
      In Generals.io this is "pass".

  LOCAL INCONSISTENCY
      A state whose g was just lowered and which has not been re-expanded.
      ARA*'s OPEN list is exactly the set of these.

  MIN-CONFLICTS
      Repair-based CSP solving: start from a complete broken assignment, fix
      the most-conflicted variable, repeat.

  METAREASONING
      Reasoning about which computation to perform, rather than which action
      to take.

  NULL WINDOW
      A search with alpha and beta one apart. It cannot return an exact
      value, only "above or below", which makes it very cheap.

  PREFIX COMMITMENT
      How many steps of a planned path you execute before re-planning.

  SIMULTANEOUS MOVES
      Both players commit for the same tick without seeing the other. Breaks
      the assumption behind plain minimax.

  TRANSPOSITION TABLE
      A hash table of positions already searched, so reaching the same
      position by a different move order costs nothing.

  WEIGHTED A*
      A* with f = g + eps*h for eps > 1. Faster, and provably within a factor
      eps of optimal.
