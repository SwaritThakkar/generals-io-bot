1. THE GAME
================================================================================


THE BOARD

the game starts on a grid of square tiles, twenty-three by twenty-three. Four kinds of tile:

  - plain tiles, which anyone can walk on and own
  - mountains, which are impassable and just sit there as walls
  - castles, which produce army for whoever owns them
  - generals, one per player, which are both a base and the thing you must defend

Each player starts owning exactly one tile: their general. Every tile you own
carries a number, which is how many army units are standing on it. Mine are
drawn red, my opponent's blue, and unowned tiles are grey.

  [FIGURE - "The board"                                        TO GENERATE
   One real mid-game position, large and clean, with callouts pointing at: a
   general (crown), an owned castle (ring), a neutral castle showing its 40-50
   garrison, a mountain, an owned tile with its army number, and an unowned
   tile. Both colours present. This is the first picture the audience ever sees
   of the game, so it must read from the back of the room - use a smaller board
   with bigger cells, not the full 23x23.
   Replaces media/fig1, which is about to become wrong.]


MOVING AND FIGHTING

Each turn - a "tick" - you pick one tile you own and one of the four directions,
and you send either all but one of its units, or exactly half of them, to the
neighbouring tile. You may also pass. One move per tick. That is the whole
action set.

Combat is resolved by subtraction, which is unusually simple for a strategy
game. The two stacks cancel one for one and whoever is larger survives with the
difference.

Concretely: my tile has 10 units and the tile to its right is my opponent's,
holding 4. I move right, sending all but one - so 9 units leave, and 1 stays
behind. Nine attackers meet four defenders. I take the tile and it now holds 5
units, which are mine. Had they held 12 instead, they would have kept the tile
with 3 left standing.

  [FIGURE - "Combat is subtraction"                            TO GENERATE
   Three panels. (a) BEFORE: my tile 10, their tile 4, arrow between them.
   (b) THE MOVE: 9 leave, 1 stays, drawn as the stack splitting. (c) AFTER: my
   tile shows 1, the captured tile shows 5 and has turned red. A smaller second
   line underneath: "had they held 12, they keep it with 3".
   The arithmetic is trivial - show it rather than say it.]


Empty tiles cost almost nothing: walk onto a neutral tile with any units at all
and it is yours. So a stack of size a can capture a-1 tiles in a row before it
runs out - one unit gets left behind on each.

  [FIGURE - "A stack walks"                                    TO GENERATE
   A single row of five tiles. A stack of 5 starts at the left and walks right
   across four neutral tiles, one panel per tick. Each tile it leaves keeps
   exactly 1 unit and turns red; the moving stack shrinks 5 -> 4 -> 3 -> 2 -> 1.
   Caption: a stack of size a captures a-1 tiles. This is the mental model for
   the entire expansion phase.]


WHERE ARMY COMES FROM

This is the part that quietly drives every decision in the game.

  - your general produces one unit every two ticks
  - each castle you own produces one unit every two ticks
  - EVERY tile you own produces one unit every fifty ticks

Neutral castles sit around the map holding forty to fifty units. Beat that
garrison and the castle is yours, producing for you for the rest of the game.

Those numbers are worth converting now, because they come back repeatedly. A
producer makes half a unit per tick. A plain tile makes one fiftieth of a unit
per tick. So one castle is worth exactly twenty-five plain tiles in production
terms - which is a fact about the rules, not an opinion about strategy.

  [FIGURE - "One castle = twenty-five tiles"                   EXISTS, needs trimming
   media/fig8, top half only: castle at 0.50 army/tick, plain tile at 0.02
   army/tick, and the equivalence underneath. Drop the 45-army spending
   comparison for now - that belongs later with Idea 4.]


The consequence is that this is a compounding game. Territory produces army,
army takes territory, and small early advantages snowball. A player who is ten
tiles ahead at turn fifty is usually much further ahead by turn two hundred.

  [FIGURE - "Compounding"                                      TO GENERATE (real data)
   Two stacked line charts from an actual self-play game in our simulator: land
   over time, and army over time, red against blue. Pick a game where one side
   pulls ahead around tick 60 and the gap widens from there; mark the moment the
   lead opens. The message is entirely visual - the curves diverge and never
   come back together.]


HOW YOU WIN

Move an army onto the enemy general and win the fight there. That is it. There
is no points system and no other victory condition.

  [FIGURE - "How you win"                                      TO GENERATE
   Small board, a red stack three tiles from the blue general, the path arrowed,
   and the capture. Keep it tiny - one idea, sitting beside two lines of text.]




BOTH PLAYERS SEE EVERYTHING

The board is fully observable. There is nothing hidden on it - both players see
every tile, every army count, and both generals, from the first tick.

That matters more than it sounds, because it is what makes the algorithms in
this course apply at all. Classical adversarial search assumes you can evaluate
the position in front of you. Here you can: what you see is what is there,
evaluation is exact rather than expected, and a question like "can my opponent
capture my general within six ticks?" has a definite yes-or-no answer rather
than a probability.

  [FIGURE - "Everything is visible"                            TO GENERATE
   The position rendered once, with a short list beside it of what is therefore
   known exactly: enemy general location, every army count, every castle
   garrison, the whole terrain. No second panel, no comparison - just the board
   and what it tells you.
   use the difference figure in /media here as well]


WHAT MAKES IT HARD

Three things, and they are what the rest of these notes are about.

The first is time. The game is a real time strategy game. Both players move together
and thus time is of key importance.

The second is the branching factor. Every tile you own with at least two units
can move in four directions, in either of two move types, so average branching factor 
turns out to be between 40 and 400!. Thus, depth is incredibly expensive for this problem.

  [FIGURE - "Branching factor"                                 EXISTS: media/fig2
   Chess (b = 35) against this game (b = 204, measured from a real position),
   each with its fan of legal moves drawn out, plus the table of how alpha-beta
   scales with depth. The solid black wedge of 204 branches beside the thin
   chess fan is the most persuasive picture in the deck - give it a full slide.]


The third is easy to miss: the reward is sparse and terminal. You win or you
lose, five hundred ticks later. Nothing tells you at tick one hundred and
thirty-seven whether that particular move was good.

  [FIGURE - "Sparse reward"                                    TO GENERATE
   A horizontal timeline of 500 ticks. Almost the whole bar is flat grey,
   labelled "no signal", with a few decision points marked by question marks. At
   the far right, a single spike: "+1 win / -1 loss". One glance should explain
   why evaluation is the hard part.]


And there is one genuine piece of hidden information, which is what stops the
game collapsing into a solved search problem: both players commit their move for
the same tick without seeing the other's. This sounds simple but has very impresive implications, 
largest of which is plain minimax is still not quite right. It quietly assumes you move second and get to see what your
opponent did - and dealing with it properly is one of the open questions in this
project.

  [FIGURE - "Simultaneous moves"                               TO GENERATE
   Two panels. (a) SEQUENTIAL, what minimax assumes: I move, then they see it
   and respond - mark this one as false. (b) SIMULTANEOUS, what actually
   happens: both moves committed face-down, revealed together. Under (b), a
   small 2x2 payoff matrix, to make the point that a node here is a matrix game
   rather than a max or a min.]

2. WHY SEARCH, AND WHY IT IS NOT A FALLBACK
================================================================================

The "constraint" on this project is that I can only use search and constraint
satisfaction — chapters three to six of Russell and Norvig. No reinforcement
learning, no neural networks, no machine learning of any kind.

To provide some context - The strongest non-learning agent on the public Generals.io (the game with fog) ladder is built entirely
out of classical optimisation — graph algorithms and dynamic programming — and
it was the best bot in the world at this game until a reinforcement learning
agent trained on four H200 GPUs beat it. Search is a serious approach here, not
a consolation prize. 

We note that the sub-problems in this game are genuinely NP-hard. Gathering scattered army optimally to one
point within a turn budget is a "rooted prize-collecting Steiner tree". Deciding
what to spend a limited number of turns on is a multiple-choice knapsack
problem. 

Shortly speaking, there is no closed form solution here. And so, when a problem has no
analytic solution and a combinatorial search space, search is what you use.

  [FIGURE - "The sub-problems are NP-hard"                     TO GENERATE
   A board with my army scattered across a dozen tiles in different amounts, my
   general marked as the root, and collection routes drawn over it - some
   branches taken, some skipped with their army greyed out. Label it as a rooted
   prize-collecting Steiner tree: prize = army on a tile, cost = ticks to reach
   it, skipping allowed. One line underneath: this is NP-hard, which is why
   there is no formula for the right move. mention the name "rooted prize-collecting steiner tree "]

3. THE PAPERS
================================================================================

I picked five papers, one per topic in the course. 

3.1  ARA*: ANYTIME A* WITH PROVABLE BOUNDS ON SUB-OPTIMALITY
     Likhachev, Gordon and Thrun. NIPS 2003.

Ordinary A* has a property that is fatal for me: it gives you nothing until it
finishes. If my half second runs out mid-search I have no answer at all, and
the tick fires regardless.

Weighted A* is the classical fix. You inflate the heuristic — f equals g plus
epsilon times h, with epsilon greater than one — which makes the search greedier
and much faster. The important part is that this comes with a theorem, not a
hope: the path you find costs at most epsilon times the optimal cost. So you
have a dial between speed and quality, and a guarantee at every setting.

  [ here you also show the theorem nicely in bold ]
  [FIGURE - "Weighted A* trades quality for speed"             TO GENERATE
   Three small grid panels side by side, same start and goal, same obstacles.
   Shade the cells each search expanded. eps=1: a wide symmetric blob, optimal
   path, many expansions. eps=2: a narrower cone aimed at the goal, slightly
   longer path. eps=3: a thin corridor, clearly suboptimal path, very few
   expansions. Label each with expansions and path cost. The visual point is
   that inflating h makes the search greedy and directional.]


So we run ARA  repeatedly with epsilon decreasing — three, then two, then one and a half — and
to make each run reuse the previous run's work rather than starting over. You
get a rough answer almost immediately, it improves the longer you let it run,
and at every moment you know exactly how far from optimal you are.

  [FIGURE - "Anytime: quality against time"                    TO GENERATE
   A step chart. x = time in milliseconds up to our 500 ms budget. y = solution
   cost, with the optimum as a dashed floor line. A descending staircase, one
   step per eps value (3, 2, 1.5, 1.2, 1), each step annotated with its
   guaranteed bound. A vertical line at 400 ms marked DEADLINE showing which
   step we would actually have been holding. Underneath: plain A* would still be
   returning nothing at that line.]


The paper also describes a way to use first iterations' finding for the next one (reduced alpha) as shown here.

  [FIGURE - "The three lists"                                  TO GENERATE
   OPEN, CLOSED and INCONS drawn as three boxes with the states flowing between
   them: expanded states move OPEN -> CLOSED; a state whose g improves after it
   was closed goes to INCONS instead of back to OPEN; and at the end of a pass
   the whole of INCONS is poured back into OPEN for the next eps. That last
   arrow is the reuse, and it is the one to highlight.]


3.2  REAL-TIME ADAPTIVE A* (RTAA*)
     Koenig and Likhachev. AAMAS 2006.

An agent that has to act now, cannot search all the way to the goal, and will be back in
a similar situation next step. The algorithm the book teaches is LRTA*, which
keeps a table of heuristic estimates and raises the estimate for the state it is
standing in whenever its neighbours turn out to be further from the goal than
expected. Over repeated visits the table becomes more accurate and the agent
stops making the same mistake twice.

The weakness of LRTA* is that it updates essentially one state per move, so on
a large map the agent has to physically walk around correcting one number at a
time. RTAA* replaces that with a bounded-depth A* search followed by an update
of *every* state that search expanded, with one subtraction each.

  [FIGURE - "LRTA* against RTAA*"                              TO GENERATE
   Same grid twice. LEFT (LRTA*): a bounded search region, with exactly one cell
   highlighted as updated. RIGHT (RTAA*): the same region with EVERY expanded
   cell highlighted. Same lookahead, same cost of search, vastly more heuristic
   information gained per move. This single comparison is the whole paper.]


Here is the justification - A* expands states
in order of increasing f, so when it stops, the smallest f remaining on the
frontier is a lower bound on the true distance from where you are standing to
the goal. Any path out of the searched region has to pass through that frontier.
So for any expanded state, the true distance to the goal is at least the
frontier's f value minus that state's g value — and that is exactly what gets
assigned as the new heuristic. It is a lower bound, so the heuristic stays
admissible and everything A* guarantees still holds. The paper also proves the
update is monotonically non-decreasing, which means the table only ever becomes
more informed and never degrades.

  [FIGURE - "Why the update is legal"                          TO GENERATE
   One grid. Mark the current state, shade the expanded region, draw the
   frontier as a dashed arc, and put s-bar (the frontier state with smallest f)
   as a filled dot. Then draw an arbitrary path from some expanded state s out
   to the goal, showing it MUST cross the arc. Beside it, the inequality
   dist(s, goal) >= f(s-bar) - g(s), which is exactly the assignment.
   The geometric argument lands better than the algebra.]

The paper is honest about the trade-off. LRTA*'s heuristic ends up better
informed for the same lookahead. RTAA* is simply so much cheaper per move that
it reaches the goal sooner in wall-clock terms. That is precisely my situation:
I am limited by time, not by number of moves.

There is one detail I want to flag because it becomes important later. The
pseudocode contains two lines that say, literally, "any integer greater than
zero" — how much to look ahead, and how many steps to commit to. The paper does
not tell you how to choose them. That gap is where a later paper lives.

  [FIGURE - "The two undefined parameters"                     TO GENERATE
   The RTAA* pseudocode block set in mono, with exactly two lines highlighted in
   amber - lookahead and movements - each annotated "any integer greater than
   zero: undefined by the paper". An arrow from each pointing forward to Idea 1
   and Idea 3. This is the hinge between the published work and my own
   contribution, so it probably deserves its own slide.]

3.3  DEPRESSION AVOIDANCE IN REAL-TIME HEURISTIC SEARCH
     Hernández and Baier. AAAI 2012.

This paper is about a specific way real-time search fails, and a very cheap fix.
There is almost no mathematics in it, which I take as a good sign — it is an
observation rather than a construction.

We define a heuristic depression as a connected region where the heuristic badly
underestimates the true cost compared to the values on the region's border. If
you picture the heuristic as a landscape where low means close to the goal, a
depression is a bowl. A greedy agent rolls in, because from outside everything
in the bowl looks close to the goal, and then it cannot get out: each move
raises one state's value slightly, so it wanders the bowl raising values one at
a time until the whole thing has been filled in. The literature calls this
scrubbing, and it can waste hundreds of moves.

  [FIGURE - "A heuristic depression"                           TO GENERATE
   Left: the heuristic as a 1-D landscape with a bowl between the agent and the
   goal, and the agent's path scribbling back and forth inside it - the
   scrubbing. Right: the same thing on the real board - a corridor of neutral
   tiles dead-ending into mountains, with the wasted path traced through it.
   The abstract picture and the concrete one, side by side.]

The fix is one sentence: prefer to move to states that are not known to be
inside a depression. And the detection is free.

Because the heuristic starts out admissible and updates only ever raise it, the
fact that you ever had to raise a state's value means the original estimate at
that state was too optimistic — which is the definition of being in a
depression. 

So detection costs one extra bit per state: remember the original
value, and mark the state if the current value has grown past it.

  [FIGURE - "Detection is free"                                TO GENERATE
   A grid where each cell shows h and, in smaller type, its original h0. Cells
   where h has been raised are hatched or marked with a dot. Caption: because
   updates only ever raise h, a raised value IS the detector - no separate test
   is needed. Then a second small panel showing the agent's route bending around
   the marked region.]


There are two ways to use the mark. Mark-and-avoid simply prefers unmarked
states when choosing where to head, falling back to marked ones only if
everything is marked. Move-to-border is softer: if you are already deep in a
depression and every neighbour is marked, aim for the states that look closest
to the edge rather than refusing to be in the bowl at all.

3.4  PROBCUT: FORWARD PRUNING IN ALPHA-BETA
     Buro.

Alpha-beta pruning skips subtrees that provably cannot affect the final choice.
That soundness is the whole point of it, and it is also what it costs: it only
prunes when it can *prove* irrelevance, and proof requires searching. The
result is that depth stays low, and depth is what actually makes a program
strong.

ProbCut's idea is to prune what is probably irrelevant. Before searching a
subtree to full depth, run a much shallower search on it. If the shallow result
comes back far enough outside the current alpha-beta window, conclude that the
deep search would almost certainly land outside too, and skip it.

The statistical part is simple. The value a deep search returns and the value a
shallow search returns are strongly correlated — a position that looks winning
after two ply usually looks winning after six. The paper models that
relationship as a straight line with Gaussian noise, and fits the slope,
intercept and standard deviation by ordinary least squares over a few thousand
positions, offline. That is regression, not machine learning in the sense the
course excludes.

  [FIGURE - "Shallow predicts deep"                            TO GENERATE (real data)
   A scatter plot from our own simulator: x = value from a 1-ply search, y =
   value from a 3-ply search, a few thousand self-play positions. Fitted line
   through it, and a shaded band at +/- sigma. This is the experiment from week
   two, so the figure doubles as a result. If the cloud is tight, ProbCut works
   here; if it is round, it does not - and either way the picture says so.]

3.5  METAREASONING: DECIDING HOW MUCH TO THINK
     O'Ceallaigh and Ruml. SoCS 2015.
     Russell and Wefald. UAI 1990 - the same Russell as the textbook.

The four papers above all answer the question "how do I search well?". This one
asks a different question: "should I be searching at all right now, and if so,
how much?"

Russell and Wefald set up the framework in 1990. Treat each possible node
expansion as an action in its own right, with a cost - the time it takes - and a
benefit, which is the probability that performing it actually CHANGES WHICH MOVE
YOU FINALLY PLAY, multiplied by how much better that move turns out to be. Then
expand the node with the highest value of computation, and stop searching when
the expected benefit of another expansion drops below its cost.

The sentence that matters is this one:

    A computation that cannot change your answer has zero value,
    no matter how deep it is.

That sounds obvious written down, and almost no game-playing program acts on it.
The standard design searches to a fixed depth, or until a fixed time limit,
regardless of whether the decision was ever in doubt.

  [FIGURE - "Value of computation"                             TO GENERATE
   Two small game trees side by side. LEFT: the root's children have wildly
   different values, so expanding any of them further cannot change which one is
   best - mark the whole subtree as "zero value, do not search". RIGHT: the
   children are nearly tied, so one more expansion could flip the answer - mark
   it "worth searching". The contrast is the entire idea, and it needs no
   equations to land.]

The 2015 paper takes this framework into real-time search and identifies two
specific decision points. What makes it fit this project so neatly is that they
are exactly the two lines RTAA* left undefined in section 3.2.

The first is the IDENTITY ACTION. An identity action keeps you in the same state
while you think longer. Should the agent act now, or stand still and deliberate?
Standard real-time search never asks - it assumes acting is always right. But if
your current best move is only marginally better than the second best, another
round of lookahead might turn up something much better, and standing still costs
only time.

They measure everything in goal achievement time, which is planning time plus
execution time. Under that metric thinking is not free, but it is frequently
cheaper than committing to a bad move and then having to undo it.

The second is PREFIX COMMITMENT. After lookahead you hold a path to the best
state you found. How much of that path do you actually execute before replanning?
The existing algorithms sit at the two extremes and neither adapts: LRTA* commits
to exactly one action, while later variants commit to the entire path to the
frontier. One action means replanning constantly, which is expensive; the whole
path means acting on stale information, which is dangerous. The paper chooses
dynamically - commit further when the plan looks robust, less when it looks
fragile.

  [FIGURE - "The two decisions"                                TO GENERATE
   Reuse the RTAA* pseudocode block from 3.2 with the same two amber lines, but
   now with each one answered. Beside "lookahead = any integer > 0" put
   DECISION 1: act now, or stand still and think? Beside "movements = any
   integer > 0" put DECISION 2: how far do I commit before replanning? Then a
   second panel showing prefix commitment concretely - a long safe path through
   my own territory queued whole, against a short path into contested territory
   committed one step at a time.]

Two things make this unusually applicable here, and they are both accidents of
the domain rather than anything clever on my part.

This game has a real identity action. Index zero on every tile is "pass" - the
agent can literally stand still and think, and the rules permit it. Most domains
in this literature have to simulate an identity action or argue by analogy.

And the game has a move queue: you can queue a path of moves in advance and the
server plays one per tick. That is prefix commitment implemented for us, in the
rules, with no work on my part.

So both of the paper's decision points have a direct, literal counterpart in the
game. That is why this direction is where I think my own contribution sits, and
sections 5 and 6 are largely about testing it.

5. THE IDEAS I WANT TO TEST
================================================================================

These are hypotheses rather than claims.

The first idea is that the value of computation is measurable in this domain
rather than merely estimable. Russell and Wefald's metareasoning framework asks
you to weigh the cost of a computation against the probability that it changes
which action you finally take, and the hard part has always been estimating that
probability before doing the computation. Here I do not have to estimate it. The
batched evaluator gives me the full one-ply score distribution in under a
millisecond, and the gap between the best and second-best move is a direct,
computed proxy for whether more thinking could change the answer. The cost side
is quantifiable too: passing costs exactly one tick of tempo, and the production
rate is fixed by the rules, so I can price a tick of thinking in army units.
Both sides of the inequality are computable, which is unusual.

The second idea is speculative pre-computation, and it is the one I am most
interested in. Production in this game is completely deterministic — after the
map is generated there is no randomness at all. So if nobody moved, the board
ten ticks from now is exactly computable from the board today. The only
uncertainty about the near future is the handful of moves each side makes, and
each move perturbs a tiny region of a five-hundred-tile board. That means I can
start searching next tick's problem during this tick, because I already know
almost all of it, and patch in the few changed tiles when the real moves arrive.
Done properly this roughly doubles my effective compute budget without making
anything faster. And it depends entirely on the game being deterministic and
fully observable: I can only extrapolate a board I can actually see.

  [FIGURE - "Idea 2: searching ahead of the clock"             EXISTS: media/fig5
   The two timelines: without pre-roll, think-then-idle; with pre-roll, the idle
   half of each tick spent searching the next one. Consider adding a second
   panel showing how few tiles actually change between consecutive boards -
   that is the fact the whole idea rests on, and it is measurable.]

The third idea is a reframing of the game's move queue. Normally the queue is
treated as a user-interface convenience. I want to treat it as a compute budget:
every move I queue is a tick of thinking time I have bought, because during a
queued move I owe the game no decision. Queue a twelve-step march home through
my own territory and I have bought six seconds of uninterrupted computation. The
commit decision then stops being about confidence and becomes a trade — how much
thinking time do I want, against how long am I willing to act on stale
information. This is the prefix-commitment decision from the metareasoning
literature, but with an interpretation those papers do not have, because their
domains do not have a queue.

  [FIGURE - "Idea 3: the queue is a compute budget"            EXISTS: media/fig6
   Twelve queued moves buying six seconds of thinking, and the robust-versus-
   fragile split underneath.]


We plan to use media/fig3-tree-regions.svg as the key backbone architecture of the project.
