## where my existing ideas landed, after reading the papers
================================================================================

obs 4  "is this np-hard or what"
       The sub-problems are. Gathering army optimally is a prize-collecting
       Steiner tree (NP-hard). Choosing what to spend a turn budget on is
       multiple-choice knapsack (NP-hard). The full game is worse than that.
       So: no closed form, and that is exactly why search is the right tool.
       Worth saying out loud - it justifies the whole project in one line.

obs 9  "branching factor is a lot, need a local search kind of thing"
       Correct, and it has a name: Large Neighbourhood Search. Destroy part of
       a plan, re-solve just that part, keep it if better, repeat until the
       clock runs out. Anytime by construction.

obs 13 "split the game by time (early/mid/end)"  -> see NEW IDEA 4 below.
       The instinct is right but hardcoding tick ranges is the weak version.

idea 1 "evaluation + neighbour generation + local search"
       Built it. 20% win rate. Loses 12/20 by general capture with land dead
       even. The reason is that `simulate` assumes the opponent passes, so an
       attack is invisible. Fix is in what we SEARCH, not how we SCORE.

idea 2 "utility of node expansion is going to come very very handy"
       This is Russell & Wefald's "value of computation" (UAI 1990), and it is
       the foundation of the entire metareasoning literature. You arrived at
       the right idea independently. See NEW IDEA 1 - our game makes it
       computable rather than merely estimable, which is unusual.

idea 4 "reduce branching factor by removing obvious branches"
       Two published versions: asymmetric action abstraction (prune the
       OPPONENT's moves hard, keep ours sharp) and ProbCut (use a cheap
       shallow search to decide whether a deep one is worth it).

idea 5 "wait for T, then act... entirely observable so I can predict the state
        at T with probability -> 1, so start computing at the start"
       THIS IS THE BEST IDEA IN THE FILE and it is not covered by any of the
       five papers. Fully developed as NEW IDEA 2. Closest published relative
       is Cserna/Ruml ICAPS 2017 (planning while acting), which supports it
       but does not do what you are describing.

idea 6 "the move queue is an inefficiency you can point out"
       It is also an opportunity - it is a native implementation of "prefix
       commitment" from the metareasoning paper. See NEW IDEA 3.

--- IDEA 1 -----------------------------------------------------------------
VALUE OF COMPUTATION IS *MEASURABLE* HERE, NOT JUST ESTIMABLE
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The hard part of Russell & Wefald's framework is estimating the benefit of
  a computation before you do it. Everyone approximates it.

  We do not have to. The JAX simulator evaluates EVERY legal move in one
  batched call, 0.6 ms. So at the start of every tick we get the full
  distribution of 1-ply scores for free.

      gap = best_score - second_best_score

  That gap is a direct, computed proxy for "could more thinking change my
  answer?". A large gap means no amount of search will change the choice.
  A small gap means the decision is genuinely open.

  And the COST side is quantifiable too. Passing (the identity action) costs
  exactly one tick of tempo, and we know the production rate:

      rho = producers/2 + land/50      army per tick

  So the cost of thinking for one more tick is about rho army, and the
  benefit is bounded by the score spread. Both sides of Russell & Wefald's
  inequality are computable in our domain.

  *** MEASURED, 2026-09-07 - and it corrected the idea. See media/fig4. ***

  I ran the scan over a full 300-tick game, sampling every 5 ticks, and the
  gap between the best and second-best move is ALMOST ALWAYS EXACTLY ZERO.
  Many moves are genuinely interchangeable - expanding into two equivalent
  empty tiles gives an identical board score. So "gap to second" is the wrong
  signal.

  What does work is the FRACTION OF MOVES TIED AT THE BEST SCORE, and it turns
  out to be strongly bimodal:

      38% of ticks    almost nothing ties  -> 1-ply has already decided,
                                              play immediately, ~3 ms
      57% of ticks    most moves tie       -> 1-ply cannot separate them,
                                              this is where the budget belongs
       5% of ticks    in between

  Because it is bimodal rather than spread out, a single threshold separates
  the two cases cleanly - no delicate tuning.

  That 38% is the headline number: better than a third of all ticks can be
  settled for 0.6 ms instead of 500 ms, before writing any search at all.

  TEST: fix total compute per game. Compare uniform allocation against
  tie-fraction-driven allocation. Same compute, different distribution.

  WHY IT MIGHT STILL FAIL: I have shown the signal is cheap and bimodal, not
  that acting on it wins games. The 57% "blind" ticks might be ones where
  deeper search does not help either.


--- IDEA 2 -----------------------------------------------------------------
SPECULATIVE PRE-COMPUTATION - my idea 5, developed
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The observation: production is fully deterministic. If nobody moved, the
  board at tick t+k is EXACTLY computable from the board at tick t. Army
  grows on a fixed schedule; nothing is random after map generation.

  So the only uncertainty about the future is the <=k moves each side makes,
  and each move perturbs a tiny region of a 529-tile board.

  Consequence: while tick t is being played out, we can already be searching
  tick t+1's problem, because we know almost all of it.

  Three concrete versions, increasing in ambition:

  (a) BASELINE PRE-ROLL. During the 0.5 s of tick t, compute the no-move
      baseline board for t+1 and start the search on it. When the real moves
      arrive, patch the few changed tiles and continue.

  (b) SEARCH-TREE REPAIR. Do not throw away the tick-t search tree. The
      opponent's move invalidates only the branches touching the tiles they
      changed. Repair those; keep the rest. This is exactly ARA*'s INCONS
      idea and min-conflicts' repair idea, applied to the SEARCH TREE ITSELF
      rather than to a path or an assignment.

  (c) COMMIT-AND-THINK. Queue a move we are confident about, then use the
      whole of the next tick - during which we are not deciding anything - to
      search the tick after that. This is where my "wait for T then act"
      idea actually pays off: we are not idling, we are running ahead.

  This effectively doubles the compute budget without any speedup, and it is
  enabled specifically by perfect information plus deterministic production.
  With fog it would be impossible - you could not predict the board because
  you could not see it.

  TEST: measure nodes searched per decision, with and without pre-roll.
  Then win rate.

  WHY IT MIGHT FAIL: engineering complexity. Repairing a search tree
  correctly is fiddly and easy to get subtly wrong. Version (a) is safe;
  attempt (b) only if (a) works.


--- IDEA 3 -----------------------------------------------------------------
THE MOVE QUEUE AS A COMPUTE BUDGET
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  The game lets you queue a path of moves; the server plays one per tick.
  Normally people treat this as a UI convenience.

  Reframe it: EVERY QUEUED MOVE IS A TICK OF THINKING TIME BOUGHT.

  If I queue a 12-step march home through my own territory, I have bought 12
  ticks x 0.5 s = 6 SECONDS of uninterrupted computation, during which I owe
  the game nothing.

  So the commit decision is not "how confident am I" but a trade:

      queue k moves  ->  gain k*0.5 s of thinking
                     ->  risk acting on stale information for k ticks

  And the risk is quantifiable: a path entirely inside our own territory with
  no enemy stack within reach cannot go wrong. A path approaching contact can.

  This is "prefix commitment" from the metareasoning paper, but with an
  interpretation the paper does not have, because their domains do not have
  a queue.

  TEST: measure average committed-path length against win rate. There should
  be an optimum - too short wastes the mechanism, too long acts blind.


--- IDEA 4 -----------------------------------------------------------------
LET THE GAME PHASE EMERGE, DO NOT HARDCODE IT
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  My observation 13 said split the game into early/mid/endgame like chess.
  Right instinct, but chess phases are defined by material and hardcoded tick
  ranges would be arbitrary here.

  Better: the phase is already visible in the 1-ply score distribution.

      wide gaps, consistently   -> expansion phase, decisions are obvious
      gaps collapse             -> contact, decisions are genuinely close
      one move scores enormously-> a forcing sequence exists

  So the phase DETECTS ITSELF from the metareasoning signal we are computing
  anyway, and there is no threshold to hand-tune. Strategy switching then
  becomes a consequence of budget allocation rather than a separate mechanism.

  TEST: plot the score gap over a whole game. If it has clear regimes, the
  idea is real and the plot is a good slide. If it is noise, drop it.


--- IDEA 5 -----------------------------------------------------------------
EVALUATION WEIGHTS DERIVED FROM THE RULES, NOT TUNED
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Straight from the production rules, no fitting:

      producer (general/castle):  1 army / 2 ticks  = 0.50 army/tick
      land tile:                  1 army / 50 ticks = 0.02 army/tick
      => one castle is worth exactly 25 land tiles of production

  Now price them. A tile costs 1 army permanently (the unit left behind).
  A neutral castle costs its garrison, 40-50.

      spend A army on land   -> A tiles     -> 0.02*A army/tick
      spend A army on castle -> 1 producer  -> 0.50   army/tick
      break-even at A = 25

  Castles cost 40-50. So on production alone, BUYING LAND BEATS BUYING A
  CASTLE BY ROUGHLY 1.8x, whenever that much free land is reachable.

  That is a falsifiable claim about a game most people play by feel, and it
  gives us evaluation weights that are DERIVED rather than tuned - which is
  what keeps the whole agent learning-free.

  TEST: two identical bots, one takes castles greedily, one never until free
  land runs out. 200 games. The interesting output is not who wins but WHERE
  THE CROSSOVER TICK IS.

  WHY IT MIGHT FAIL: it ignores that castles are defensible and tiles are
  recapturable for 2 army each. The experiment will tell us how much that
  matters.


--- IDEA 6 -----------------------------------------------------------------
TIME-VARYING VALUE OF LAND (the 50-tick cycle)
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Every owned tile pays +1 army every 50 ticks, on a global schedule. So a
  tile captured at tick 49 pays out one tick later; a tile captured at tick
  51 waits 49 ticks for its first payment.

  The marginal value of a tile therefore depends on where we are in the cycle:

      value(tile at time t)  proportional to  1 / (1 + (50 - (t mod 50)))

  This is derivable, it costs nothing to compute, and it predicts a real
  rhythm: expand hard just before the tick, consolidate just after.

  Strong players do this by instinct. We would be doing it because the
  evaluation function says so.

  TEST: add the cycle term, run 200 games. Also check whether the resulting
  behaviour visibly changes in the viewer - that is a nice thing to show.


--- IDEA 7 -----------------------------------------------------------------
DECOMPOSE THE SIMULTANEOUS-MOVE MATRIX BY SPATIAL INDEPENDENCE
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Both players move at the same instant, so a game-tree node is really a
  400 x 400 matrix of (my move, their move) pairs. Unmanageable.

  But most of those pairs do not interact. My move on the left edge and their
  move on the right edge affect disjoint tiles - the outcome is just the sum
  of the two independent outcomes.

  So partition the board into CONFLICT ZONES - regions where both players have
  moves touching the same tiles - solve a small independent matrix per zone,
  and add the values.

      n zones, k moves each side  ->  n * k^2   instead of   k^n * k^n

  This is AIMA section 6.5 (independent subproblems in CSPs) applied to a
  game matrix instead of a constraint graph. The connection between chapters
  5 and 6 is the interesting part.

  TEST: measure how many independent zones actually occur in real positions.
  If most positions have 3+ zones, the idea pays. If everything is one big
  contested blob by midgame, it does not.

  WHY IT MIGHT FAIL: exactly that - late-game positions may be fully
  connected. Measure the zone count distribution BEFORE building it.


--- IDEA 8 -----------------------------------------------------------------
PROVE SAFETY, THEN STOP DEFENDING
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Because there is no fog and the dynamics are deterministic, we can ask a
  question no fogged agent can:

      "Can the opponent force a capture of my general within k ticks?"

  This is a small AND-OR search (AIMA 4.3): my moves are OR nodes, their
  replies are AND nodes (must hold against all). Restricted to k <= 6 and to
  enemy stacks actually within reach, the tree is small.

  The payoff is not the answer itself, it is what it licenses:

      provably safe for k ticks  ->  spend the ENTIRE budget on attack,
                                     and skip the defensive CSP constraints
      not provably safe          ->  override whatever the evaluation said

  A proof beats a heuristic opinion. And it is another instance of the same
  principle - a cheap certainty telling us not to waste expensive computation.

  TEST: how often is the safety check conclusive? If it is almost always
  "safe", the check is nearly free information. If it is usually inconclusive,
  the tree is too big and the idea needs a smaller k.


--- IDEA 9 -----------------------------------------------------------------
ADAPTIVE BEAM WIDTH FROM THE SCORE DISTRIBUTION
---------------------------------------------------------------------------

  Beam search (AIMA 4.1) keeps the best k candidates. Everyone picks k by
  hand.

  We get all 400 scores in 0.6 ms, so we can pick k from the data: sort the
  scores, find the largest gap in the top of the distribution, and cut there.
  Sometimes 3 moves are clearly ahead; sometimes 40 are tied. Fixed k is
  wrong in both cases.

  Small idea, nearly free, and it makes the beam width a consequence of the
  position rather than a magic number in the code.
